1 · The one question that decides the method

In Session 3, the waterfall team met the clerk in week 12. That was not carelessness. It was the correct decision given the cost of the alternative. In 1970, putting working software in front of a user meant machine time, punched cards and an operator — so the cheapest way to get a requirement right was to think very hard before writing any code.

That cost has collapsed. Everything that people call “agile” follows from the collapse. So before adopting any method, ask one question:

The question

How much does it cost to put something that works in front of a real user, and to act on what they say?

Figure 1. Agile is not newer, faster or more modern. It is the rational answer to one question about cost. Where the answer changes, the correct method changes with it — which is why SANTE-BASSAM will be run both ways at the same time.
Applied to SANTE-BASSAM
Price the feedback loop before choosing the method
Cheap — the workflow
The post is twenty minutes away. The clerk will sit with you on a Friday morning. One round of feedback on the registration screen costs one morning and one bus fare. Iterate.
Expensive — the law and the contract
The patient identifier format, the consent wording and the sync contract are used by 6,000 records and a second device. Getting them wrong costs a migration, or a legal problem. Specify once.

2 · The Agile Manifesto, in full

In February 2001, seventeen practitioners who had each been running projects in a lighter way met at a ski lodge in Snowbird, Utah. They disagreed about almost everything except four sentences. Those four sentences are the whole of the Manifesto, and they are short enough to read in class. Read them before anyone tells you what they mean.

Manifesto for Agile Software Development — the four values, verbatim
Individuals and interactions

processes and tools
Working software
over
comprehensive documentation
Customer collaboration

contract negotiation
Responding to change

“That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.”
Beck, K. et al., Manifesto for Agile Software Development, 2001. The authors place the declaration under a notice permitting it to be copied freely, provided it is copied in its entirety. It is reproduced here in full for that reason.
The sentence everyone drops
The closing line is not a politeness. It is the load-bearing part. Each value is a trade, not an abolition: the items on the right still have value, and a team that reads only the left-hand column produces exactly the caricature that gave agile its bad name — no documents, no plan, no contract, and a project that cannot be handed to anyone.

So the useful way to read each line is as a pair of questions: what does the trade buy me, and what do I still owe?

The valueWhat the trade buysWhat you still owe
Individuals and interactions
over processes and tools		 The clerk says the search screen confuses her. Two students go to the desk on Friday with the tablet. No ticket, no triage queue, no three-day delay. A note in the repository the same day. An interaction that leaves no trace is a conversation, not engineering — the next student cannot inherit it.
Working software
over comprehensive documentation		 In week 6 the clerk types into a real registration screen rather than reading a forty-page specification. Her reaction is the requirement. The lightweight requirements document of Session 9. The identifier format and the sync contract cannot be discovered by typing into a screen.
Customer collaboration
over contract negotiation		 In week 8 the nurse asks for a follow-up list. It goes into the backlog and something else moves down. Nobody argues about whether it was in scope. One written non-negotiable. Whatever else is cut, no record is lost in a power cut ships in version 1.0. Collaboration without a fixed floor is drift.
Responding to change
over following a plan		 A paper prototype in week 5 shows that searching by the mother's name is essential. It enters sprint 3; card printing leaves. A goal that does not move inside the sprint. Changing direction every day is not responsiveness; it is the absence of a decision.
The mistake to expect from yourselves Every student team in this course has at some point justified skipping a test, a note or a requirements document by citing the Manifesto. None of the four values mentions tests, and the right column never says “worthless”. If your use of the Manifesto always happens to save you work, you are quoting half of it.

3 · The unit of work: the user story

Before we can run a sprint, we need something a sprint can consume. The requirements you wrote in earlier sessions look like this:

A requirement statement — correct, and unusable for planning
SR-1.1  The system shall allow a clerk to register a patient using the fields
        recorded on the existing paper card.

That sentence is fine for a contract and useless for a two-week plan. It has no owner, no size, no benefit, and the clerk cannot look at it and tell you whether it is worth building first. A user story fixes all four.

Definition — user story

A user story is one piece of work, written from the point of view of the person who wants it, small enough to finish inside a sprint and concrete enough for that person to judge when it is done. It follows a fixed three-clause template:

As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>.

A story is a promise of a conversation, not a specification. The conversation happens when the story is planned; what the conversation produces is written underneath as acceptance criteria.

Figure 2. The three clauses and what each one is for. Students almost always write the first two and omit the third — and then cannot explain why one story should be built before another.

3.1 Testing a story: INVEST

Six letters, six quick questions. A story that fails one of them is not forbidden; it is a warning that planning will be painful.

I
Independent
Can it be built without waiting for another unbuilt story? If not, order the two.
N
Negotiable
Is there room to discuss how? A story that names a widget has already closed the conversation.
V
Valuable
Does the benefit clause name something a real person gains? “so that the database is normalised” fails.
E
Estimable
Do you know enough to size it? If not, the honest answer is a spike, not a bigger number.
S
Small
Can the six of you finish it inside one sprint, with room for the other stories? If not, split it.
T
Testable
Can you write Given/When/Then for it now? If you cannot, you do not yet know what it means.
Applied to SANTE-BASSAM
One story repaired, one story worked in full
S-05 · first draft
“As a user, I want a better search, so that the system is easier to use.”

Fails V (“easier to use” is not a benefit anyone can weigh against another story), fails T (there is no test for “better”), and fails the role clause (“a user” is nobody). It is not a small story badly written — it is three different stories that have not been separated yet.

S-05 · repaired
As a clerk, I want to find a patient by the mother’s name and village, so that I can serve mothers who arrive without a card.
Acceptance criteria · traces to SR-1.1, SR-1.2
AC1
Given a mother’s name and a village, when the clerk searches, then every matching patient is listed, those in the named village first.
AC2
Given a name misspelled by one letter, when the clerk searches, then the correct patient still appears in the first five results.
AC3
Given the full register of 6,000 patients, when the clerk searches, then results appear in under two seconds on the desk laptop.

INVEST check. I — needs only the local store, finished in sprint 0. N — nothing here says how ranking works. V — mothers without cards are roughly a fifth of arrivals. E — comparable to work already done. S — fits a sprint alongside two others. T — AC1–AC3 are already three system tests.

3.2 Sizing: story points and velocity

Every team that estimates in hours discovers the same two things. Students are bad at estimating hours, and an hour from a tired student in week 12 is not an hour from a fresh one in week 5. So agile teams estimate relative size instead, and let arithmetic supply the rest.

Definitions — points and velocity

A story point is a unit of relative size: effort, complexity and uncertainty rolled into one number. Points are meaningful only by comparison. Pick one small, well-understood story, call it 2, and size everything else against it, using only the values 1 · 2 · 3 · 5 · 8 · 13. The gaps widen deliberately: nobody can tell a 7 from an 8, and pretending otherwise wastes the meeting.

Velocity is the total number of points a team actually completed in a sprint — completed to the Definition of Done, not “nearly finished”. It is measured, never promised. After one or two sprints it becomes the only honest answer to “how much can you take on?”

PointsWhat it means for your six-student teamExample from SANTE-BASSAM
1–2Understood, small, no surprises expected.Show the patient’s village on the result row.
3A day or so of pair work; one clear piece of behaviour.S-06 — search result under two seconds.
5Several parts, but all of them understood.S-04 — register a patient with the paper-card fields.
8Large, or containing something nobody has done before.S-05 — search with ranking and misspellings.
13Too big to plan. Split it, or spend a timeboxed spike first.“Offline sync” before it was split into S-07, S-08, S-09.
Why not just say hours? Because points are a property of the work and hours are a property of the week. Relative sizes stay true when exams arrive; hours do not. Velocity then converts points into calendar reality automatically — if the team completes fewer points during exam weeks, the plan absorbs it without anyone having to re-estimate a single story.
Six people will not finish 50% more than four Your team is six, and sprint 0 measured 16 points. Do not scale that to 24 because you have more people. Adding people adds communication paths, not output: four people have six pairwise channels, six people have fifteen. Some of the extra capacity is spent on coordination, some on the second person now waiting to have their work reviewed. Plan with the number you measured, let sprint 1 measure it again, and adjust from evidence. Friday’s session shows what actually happens to a six-person team that starts more work than it finishes.

4 · Scrum: a framework for scheduling feedback

Scrum is the most widely used agile framework, and the most widely misunderstood. It is worth being blunt about what it is before listing its parts.

One rule for the whole framework
Scrum schedules feedback. It says nothing about how to write code. Every one of its five events exists to create a fixed, unavoidable moment at which somebody inspects something: the review inspects the product, the retrospective inspects the process, the daily inspects the plan. That is the entire contribution. Teams that adopt Scrum and keep bad engineering habits get feedback on schedule about software they cannot safely change — and conclude that agile does not work. Friday’s session supplies the missing half.

Scrum has eleven parts, and they come in three kinds. Take them one kind at a time, and for each ask the same two questions: who owns it, and what does it inspect?

4.1 The sprint — the container everything else sits in

Definition — sprint
A sprint is a fixed period, one to four weeks, at the end of which there is an increment of working software. Three things are fixed the moment it begins: its length, its goal, and the fact that it does not get extended. If the work does not fit, the work goes back to the backlog — the sprint never moves. That single piece of rigidity is what makes velocity measurable and what makes the review date credible.

4.2 Three accountabilities — who is answerable for what

Product Ownerone person, not a committee
Answerable for the value of what gets built. Owns the ordering of the product backlog and has the final word on it. Says what and why; never how. SANTE-BASSAM: one student, acting as proxy for the nurse. The nurse cannot attend planning, so someone orders the backlog daily on her behalf and answers to her at each fortnightly review, where she has the final say.
Scrum Mastera facilitator, not a manager
Answerable for the process working. Runs the events to time, removes blockers, and protects the sprint goal from mid-sprint additions. Has no authority to assign work. SANTE-BASSAM: a second student, rotating each sprint. Rotation stops one person quietly becoming the manager. Five sprints and six of you means the rotation does not divide evenly — agree the order in sprint 1 and write it down, rather than discovering it in week 9.
Developerseveryone who builds it
Answerable for how the work is done and for meeting the Definition of Done. They, not the Product Owner, decide how much fits in a sprint. SANTE-BASSAM: all six of you, the Product Owner and Scrum Master included. Those two are accountabilities held alongside development work, not jobs: everyone on this team writes code and everyone tests, in every sprint. A six-person team that lets two people stop building is a four-person team with extra meetings.

4.3 Three artifacts — and the distinction that causes the most confusion

Product backlogowned by the Product Owner
The single ordered list of everything that might ever be built, expressed as user stories. It is never complete and never frozen: items are added, removed, re-ordered and re-sized at any time. Ordering is by value and risk, not by ease. Only the top of the list needs to be detailed. SANTE-BASSAM: sixteen stories, S-01 to S-16, ordered against the risk register from Session 4.
Sprint backlogowned by the Developers
What the team selected for this sprint and how they intend to build it — three things together: the sprint goal (one sentence), the stories chosen from the top of the product backlog, and the tasks those stories were broken into. It is the team’s own plan, and only the team changes it, daily, during the sprint. SANTE-BASSAM: for sprint 1 — one goal sentence, three stories totalling sixteen points, and the tasks underneath them. Worked in full in §5.3.
Incrementowned by the Developers
The working software at the end of the sprint — everything completed in this sprint plus everything completed in all previous ones, still working together. An item belongs to the increment only if it meets the Definition of Done. SANTE-BASSAM: the version installed on the desk laptop at the review, which the clerk then keeps using until the next one.
Product backlog vs. sprint backlog — the difference in one line The product backlog is everything that might be built, ordered by the Product Owner, and it outlives every sprint. The sprint backlog is the slice the Developers pulled off the top for the next two weeks, plus their plan for it, and it is thrown away when the sprint ends. Different contents, different owner, different lifetime. Confusing them is the single most common error in this unit’s coursework.
Figure 3. The loop. Read it left to right once, then follow the gold arrow back: the review is not the end of the process, it is the input to the next ordering of the product backlog. The increment is the only artifact the customer ever sees.

4.4 Five events — each one a scheduled inspection

EventTimeboxWhat happensWhat it inspects, and what comes out
The Sprint2 weeks The container for the other four. Begins the moment planning ends. Nothing — it is the period itself. Out: an increment.
Sprint Planning≤ 90 min The Product Owner proposes a goal; the Developers pull stories off the top of the product backlog until they reach their velocity, then break them into tasks. Inspects the product backlog. Out: the sprint backlog.
Daily Scrum15 min, standing Finished since yesterday · doing today · blocked by what. Nothing else is discussed here; problems get a separate conversation afterwards. Inspects progress against the sprint goal. Out: today’s plan, and a written blocker.
Sprint Review≤ 60 min The customer uses the increment. The team watches and writes; it does not demonstrate and it does not explain. Inspects the product. Out: a re-ordered product backlog.
Retrospective≤ 45 min The team alone. What to keep, what to change — about how they work, not about what they built. Inspects the process. Out: one change, with an owner, in the next sprint backlog.

4.5 The Definition of Done

Definition — Definition of Done
The team’s written, shared standard for what “finished” means. It applies to every story, it is agreed once and changed deliberately, and it lives in the repository where anyone can read it. Work that does not meet it is not part of the increment, does not count toward velocity, and cannot be shown at the review.
Why this is the element that decides whether the rest works
Without a written Definition of Done, each story is finished to a different standard, so the points are not comparable, so velocity is a fiction, so planning is guesswork, so the review date is a hope. Every other part of Scrum depends on this one paragraph being real.

5.3 The sprint backlog for sprint 1

This is the artifact students most often get wrong, so here it is in its three layers, in the order sprint planning produces them. Goal first, then stories, then tasks — never the other way round.

One sentence. It names a person, a date and something observable. It says nothing about screens, tables or code — if the team finds a cheaper way to achieve it, the goal still stands.

Notice the first and second items. The conversation with the clerk and the test both come before the screen. That ordering is not Scrum — Scrum is silent about it. It is the engineering half, and it is Friday’s session.

5.4 The team’s Definition of Done

dod.md — in the repository, agreed by all six students
A story is DONE when, and only when:

  1.  it is merged to main through a pull request reviewed by someone who did not write it
  2.  every new function has unit tests, and the whole suite is green in CI on the merge commit
  3.  each acceptance criterion has been demonstrated on the target device
  4.  new fields are in the data dictionary; new interfaces are in the sync contract
  5.  the traceability matrix is updated: story -> requirement -> tests
  6.  the nurse or the clerk can use it without a developer explaining what to click

Six lines, and every one of them is a line some team has learned the hard way. Item 3 is the one that will bite you in sprint 2: “the target device” stops meaning the laptop on the desk and starts meaning a tablet in a village with no network.

Session 5 Takeaway
Agile is what a team does when it can afford to be wrong cheaply. The Manifesto states four trades and, in the sentence everyone drops, refuses to abolish either side of them. The user story is the unit of work small enough to finish and concrete enough for a real person to judge; points size it and velocity converts points into calendar truth. Scrum then schedules the feedback — three accountabilities, three artifacts, five events — and gives your team one thing the waterfall team of Session 3 did not have: Friday 16 October, the day the clerk touches the software.
